Introduction

Kerala holds an unusual place in development studies. For decades it has served as the standard example of "good health at low cost". The explanation most scholars favour is public action. Early investment in literacy, a dense network of public facilities, and sustained political pressure to keep those facilities staffed and used are usually treated as the foundations of that record.

The data in this story do not measure health outcomes, so they cannot test that claim directly.1 What they do capture is one of the mechanisms the argument depends on: the reach of the public system. Kerala runs 985 primary health centres for an estimated 35,758,189 people.2

Two terms used throughout. A primary health centre (PHC) is the first level of the government health system with a doctor, typically serving one or a few panchayats. A population-per-unit ratio divides a district's population by its number of PHCs, beds or doctors. It shows how many people share each unit, so a higher number means thinner capacity.

On these measures, one PHC in Kerala serves 36,303 people on average, one government hospital bed serves 928, and one government doctor serves 5,570. Read at the state level, the figures describe a public system with wide reach, which is consistent with the Kerala model argument. The district figures are less reassuring.

Averages that hide a two-to-one gap

The Government of Kerala's health policy sets a norm of one PHC for every 30,000 people. The state average of 36,303 already sits above that line, and only 2 of 14 districts meet it: Pathanamthitta (23,046) and Idukki (23,654).

At the other end, Malappuram's 103 PHCs serve 4,846,282 people, or 47,051 per centre. That is about 1.3 times the state average and about double the Pathanamthitta figure. Kozhikode follows at 44,171.

The same districts appear at the bottom of the bed and doctor rankings. Malappuram has 1,861 people per government hospital bed, almost exactly twice the state average of 928 and 3.2 times Pathanamthitta's 589. Malappuram (7,437) and Kozhikode (7,245) also have the most people per government doctor, against a state average of 5,570.

Reading the gap as a Malabar question

Most discussion of these figures stops at Malappuram. The district is the extreme case, but the gap is easier to understand at the scale of the region. In this story, Malabar means the six northern districts of Kasaragod, Kannur, Wayanad, Kozhikode, Malappuram and Palakkad. This approximates the British-era Malabar district, whose boundaries do not match today's districts exactly.7

Malabar holds 45.8% of Kerala's estimated population but 42.7% of its PHCs. Taken together, its 421 PHCs serve 16,361,782 people: 38,864 per centre, compared with 34,391 in the other eight districts.4

The regional gap compounds when converted into facilities. If every district were brought up to the 30,000 norm, the twelve districts above it would need 234 more PHCs between them. 127 of those, or 54%, would be in Malabar: 59 in Malappuram, 37 in Kozhikode and 19 in Palakkad.

Run the same sum at state level and the shortfall falls to 207, because the spare capacity in Idukki and Pathanamthitta offsets part of the gap elsewhere. In practice, a PHC in the Pathanamthitta hills cannot serve a family in Malappuram. The district-level figure is the more realistic measure of need.

Malabar is not uniform

The regional averages hide a sharp internal split. Three Malabar districts, Kasaragod, Kannur and Wayanad, are better than the state average on all three measures. Kannur's 30,643 people per PHC is the third-lowest ratio in Kerala, and Wayanad has the third-lowest population per bed (638) and the second-lowest per doctor (3,930).

The weight of the gap sits in what this story calls central Malabar: Malappuram, Kozhikode and Palakkad. These are also Malabar's three most populous districts. Together they hold 11,328,507 people, 31.7% of the state, with 264 PHCs, or 42,911 people per centre. Palakkad's PHC ratio (36,679) is close to the state average, but its bed (1,111) and doctor (6,722) ratios are the fourth- and third-highest in Kerala.

Nor is the south immune. Kollam trails the average on all three measures, with 40,112 people per PHC, 1,158 per bed and 6,290 per doctor. Thrissur, which borders Malappuram and Palakkad, does the same (38,097; 964; 6,293). Ernakulam (38,656 per PHC) and Thiruvananthapuram (37,798) are also above the PHC average, though both have better-than-average bed ratios (787 and 721). Alappuzha (32,784; 632; 4,760) and Kottayam (30,677; 706; 4,701) do well on all three. Idukki is the one district with a split profile among the best performers: it has the second-lowest PHC ratio but an above-average 961 people per bed.

What the pattern suggests, and what it does not

The three measures move together. Districts that rank poorly on PHCs tend to rank poorly on doctors and beds as well. A Spearman rank correlation, which scores from −1 to 1 how closely two rankings agree, is 0.84 between the PHC and doctor rankings, 0.69 between PHCs and beds, and 0.85 between beds and doctors. With only 14 districts these figures are descriptive rather than conclusive, but they suggest one shared driver rather than three unrelated shortfalls.

Several explanations fit this pattern, and the data cannot separate them.

  • Facilities may not have kept pace with population. The most stretched districts are the most populous ones. Facility networks change slowly, so if population grew faster in some districts than others, their ratios would worsen without any active disinvestment. The dataset has no time series, so this cannot be checked here.8
  • History may matter. Travancore and Cochin, the princely states that became southern and central Kerala, are usually credited with earlier public investment in schools and health services than British-ruled Malabar. A legacy of that difference is plausible, but nothing in these figures tests it.
  • Ratios favour sparse, hilly districts by design. Idukki, Wayanad and Pathanamthitta are hilly. A facility there may serve fewer people but a larger area, and planners commonly allow smaller catchments in such terrain. A low population-per-PHC figure in a hill district is not the same as easier access.
  • Facilities are counted where they stand, not where their patients live. Large hospitals draw patients across district lines. Thiruvananthapuram's better-than-average bed (721) and doctor (5,158) ratios, alongside an above-average PHC ratio, would be consistent with tertiary care concentrated in the capital, though the data do not show patient flows.
  • The private sector is outside the frame. These ratios cover government capacity only. If some districts lean more on private hospitals, total capacity there may be less stretched than the ratios imply. For households that cannot pay private fees, the public ratio remains the relevant one.

Two further limits apply. These are inputs, not outcomes: a thinner network does not by itself show worse health. And the data are only broken down by district. They cannot speak to differences by income, gender, rural or urban residence, or community, all of which would sharpen the picture.5

Excellence on average, inequality in distribution

Kerala's state averages describe a public health network with real reach. The district figures show that this reach is unevenly shared. The gap between the best- and worst-served districts is roughly two to one on PHCs (23,046 against 47,051) and doctors (3,754 against 7,437), and more than three to one on beds (589 against 1,861). The thinnest coverage is concentrated in central Malabar, where nearly a third of Kerala's people live.

The data point to four implications for policy.

  • Plan to the district, not the average. A state average that sits near a norm can coexist with millions of people well outside it. Norms are more useful when monitored district by district.
  • Treat central Malabar as a priority for new capacity. On the norm arithmetic, Malappuram, Kozhikode and Palakkad account for 115 of the 234 PHCs needed. They also hold the three highest doctor ratios and three of the four highest bed ratios.
  • Match facilities with people. Because PHC, bed and doctor shortfalls move together, adding buildings without posts, or posts without beds, is unlikely to close the gap.
  • Fill the evidence gaps. District outcome data, private-sector capacity, sanctioned versus filled posts, and breakdowns below the district would let the Kerala model claim be tested against distribution as well as averages.

The Kerala model rests on the idea that public provision, widely spread, produces good health at modest cost. These figures suggest the provision is real but not evenly spread. Whether that unevenness shows up in health outcomes is the next question the data need to answer.

Sources & notes

Sources: Health at Glance 2024; CAG Report on Public Health 2024; List of modern medical institutions 2024-25. Figures as compiled in PRI's health slides (pp. 32–34). Government of Kerala health policy norm of 30,000 people per PHC as stated in the same slides.

  1. No outcome data. The dataset contains no health outcome indicators (for example, infant mortality or life expectancy) and no comparison with other Indian states. The opening section therefore states the Kerala model thesis as framing from the literature; it is not tested here. Citations and comparative figures should be added before publication if the argument is to rest on them.
  2. Population. District populations are labelled "Population 2024". India has not published census figures for 2024, so these are presumably projections; the method and source of the projection are not stated.
  3. Source attribution. Three publications are cited jointly for all three indicators. Which publication supplied which figure, and the reference date of each count, is not specified.
  4. Derived figures. Regional aggregates, shares, the shortfall arithmetic, the index and the correlations are PRI calculations from the district data. Bed and doctor counts are not given, only ratios; regional bed and doctor figures are back-calculated. Implied beds sum to a state ratio of 928, matching the published average. Implied doctors give about 5,689 people per doctor statewide against a published 5,570, which suggests some doctors are not attributed to districts or a different population base was used. The published averages are used wherever possible.
  5. Dimensions not available. The data are disaggregated by district only. No breakdown by income, gender, rural/urban residence or community is available, so those dimensions are not analysed.
  6. Scope of "government hospitals". The source does not define which institutions are counted for beds and doctors (for example, whether medical college hospitals are included). Private facilities are excluded throughout.
  7. Definition of Malabar. Six districts: Kasaragod, Kannur, Wayanad, Kozhikode, Malappuram, Palakkad. The historical Malabar district and today's districts do not align exactly (parts of present-day Thrissur and Palakkad had different histories). The Malabar comparison chart lets readers exclude Palakkad: on that five-district definition, Malabar has 39,409 people per PHC against 34,687 elsewhere.
  8. No time series. All figures are a single snapshot, so trends cannot be shown or tested.
  9. Spelling. The source uses both "Kasaragode" and "Kasaragod"; this story uses Kasaragod.
